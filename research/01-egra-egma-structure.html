EGRA and EGMA structure

Subtasks, timing, stop rules, Urdu adaptations, benchmarks, automated-scoring literature

EGRA / EGMA structure, Urdu adaptations, digital scoring and reliability practice

Purpose: reference document for the AI-assisted, coach-administered EGRA (English + Urdu) and EGMA design. Target: whole battery per child in ~5 min, 5 children in 30 min. Prepared: 2026-09-15. Every factual claim carries a source URL. Items not confirmed against a primary source are marked unverified.

1. EGRA canonical battery (RTI Toolkit 2nd ed., 2016)

1.1 Which subtasks are "core"

The Toolkit's common instrument for letter-based orthographies has six subtasks, four of them core: (1) listening comprehension, (2) letter identification (names or sounds), (3) nonword reading, (4) oral reading fluency (ORF) with reading comprehension. The two "most often included" additions are phonemic awareness (initial-sound identification) and familiar-word reading. Dictation, maze and cloze are optional; vocabulary is optional and untimed and "has not yet been through the same expert-panel review and validation process as the other subtasks" (Toolkit pp. 39–41). The Toolkit recommends keeping the count "around six" to control fatigue.

1.2 Universal administration rules (Toolkit protocol annex, pp. ~200–215)

  • Timed subtasks are 60 s and time-limited: the timer starts when the child reads the first item; on a tablet the screen flashes red at 60 s. If the child finishes early, the assessor records time remaining.
  • Hesitation rule: on timed reading grids, stay silent; if the child hesitates 3 s, point to the next item, say "Please go on", mark the skipped item incorrect.
  • Self-corrections count as correct.
  • Early-stop ("auto-stop") rule: if the child gets no correct response in the first line — 10 items for letter/syllable grids, 5 items for nonword/familiar-word grids, the first line of the passage for ORF — say "Thank you", discontinue, tick the "discontinued" box. The child scores zero on that subtask and is coded auto-stopped (Toolkit §10.3, p. 109; Dubeck & Gove Table 1).
  • Untimed oral items (initial sound, listening comprehension): no response after 3 s → mark "no response" and move on. Comprehension questions: no response after 10 s → move on, do not repeat.
  • Incorrect vs no-response must be distinguished at item level; per-minute rates cannot be computed otherwise (Toolkit §10.3).

1.3 The fluency formula (exact, Toolkit §10.3, p. 109)

Subtask_per_minute = Subtask_score / (Time given for subtask − subtask_time_remain) × 60

where Subtask_score = items correct, Time given is normally 60 s, and subtask_time_remain is seconds left if the child finished early (0 if timed out). Only children who finish early have a rate different from their raw count. Units: clpm (letter names), clspm (letter sounds), cnwpm (nonwords), cwpm (familiar words or passage), csspm (syllables).

1.5 Administration scripts (structure)

Every subtask follows the same script skeleton in the Toolkit annex: (a) show stimulus / read directions; (b) two worked practice items with corrective feedback ("Good, the sound of this letter is /s/" / "The sound of this letter is /s/"); (c) "When I say Begin, start here [point], go across the page, point to each letter and tell me…"; (d) start timer on first response; (e) mark with slash, bracket after last item at 60 s, record time remaining; (f) early-stop line. Prompts such as "Please tell me the sound, not the name" may be given only once. Full Timor-Leste (Tetum) and English scripts are reproduced in the Toolkit annex.

1.6 Per-subtask time

The Toolkit gives no per-subtask timings, only totals (see §3). Working estimate used in this document: each timed grid ≈ 60 s test + 30–45 s instructions/practice ≈ 90–105 s; ORF+comprehension ≈ 2–2.5 min; listening comprehension ≈ 1.5 min; initial sounds ≈ 1–1.5 min; dictation ≈ 2 min. These are estimates, not Toolkit figures.

2. EGMA canonical battery (RTI EGMA Toolkit, March 2014)

Core EGMA = six subtests (eight instruments because addition/subtraction each have Level 1 and Level 2). All are orally administered; "no student writing is required. Answers to all items are orally transmitted" — but paper/pencil and counters are available and the assessor records the strategy used (EGMA Toolkit pp. 12, 20–24). For untimed subtests, the assessor prompts the child to move on after 5 s of no response and covers not-yet-reached items with a sheet of paper. Children are reminded to point at the item they are answering so the assessor can align tablet and stimulus. Approximate administration length: 20 minutes, realistic field planning 25 min per child including swap-over (EGMA Toolkit pp. 12, 50).

Older/expanded EGMA variants (EGMA Toolkit pp. 25–27): the 2008–09 "EGMA 5" and pilot versions included Counting fluency (60 s), One-to-one correspondence, Shape recognition, Shape attributes, Pattern/number extension, Multiplication/Division; the pilot "took much longer than anticipated (well over 20 minutes)", which is why the Core EGMA was cut to the six subtests above. Shape/pattern items are therefore not in the 2014 Core; some country instruments retain them. Tanzania's national EGMA (2016) used five subtests: A/S Level 1 (timed), A/S Level 2, Missing Number, Word Problems (TZ 2016 p. 18).

Abbreviated / core subsets in practice - Toolkit "core four": listening comprehension, letter identification, nonword reading, ORF + comprehension (Toolkit p. 39). - Sindh 2024 (Grade 2, N = 7,248): five subtasks — phoneme awareness (10 untimed), nonword reading (60 s), expressive vocabulary (10 pictures, untimed), passage reading (60 s) + 5 questions, listening comprehension (3 questions). Letter naming (ceiling), syllables, phoneme segmentation (floor), familiar words, dictation and maze were deliberately dropped (Sindh 2024 pp. 6–7). - Tanzania national: 5 Kiswahili subtasks, 10–15 min (above). - Kenya Tusome / Tanzania benchmarking use only ORF + comprehension (and nonwords) as "benchmark subtasks" (TZ 2016 Table 5; ACR-Asia p. 17). - ASER (Pakistan/India) is the shortest proxy (see §4.4): 5 letters, 5 words, one paragraph, one story; ~10 min with math.

Implication for the 5-minute target: timed EGRA subtasks alone are 60 s each; three timed reading grids + ORF + comprehension + a two-item EGMA timed pair already exceed 5 min before instructions. A 5-min battery must be a subset (e.g., ORF+comprehension, one decoding grid, Number ID, A/S Level 1) or use shortened time windows — which breaks comparability with published cwpm benchmarks unless re-normed.

4. Urdu EGRA in Pakistan (plus Sindhi, Pashto; Kiswahili briefly)

4.1 Programmes and datasets

  • USAID Pakistan Reading Project (PRP, 2013–2020; IRC/Creative/World Learning): 1.7 M grade 1–2 students, 27,000+ teachers; materials in seven language variants (Urdu, two Sindhi dialects, two Pashto dialects, Balochi, Brahui) but delivered "primarily in Urdu and Sindhi" (Learning at Scale brief, https://learningatscale.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Learning-at-Scale-Findings-Brief-Pakistan-Reading-Program-for-distribution.pdf). PRP's baseline instrument was "based on the EGRA and adapted to the Pakistan level 3 and level 5 reading curriculum" (ReliefWeb, https://reliefweb.int/report/pakistan/testing-readers-early-grades-pakistan). Third-party EGRA endlines by MSI exist for 2020 — PRP 2020 EGRA Endline (https://pdf.usaid.gov/pdf_docs/PA00Z7MW.pdf, https://pdf.usaid.gov/pdf_docs/PA00ZJJG.pdf, Balochistan https://pdf.usaid.gov/pdf_docs/PA00Z7MQ.pdf) — the USAID DEC host was unreachable from this session, so their numbers are unverified here. A widely repeated PRP claim that Sindhi/Urdu ORF rose "from 30 cwpm in 2014 to 57 in 2017" appears in search summaries of Creative Associates' prize story (https://www.creativeassociatesinternational.com/story/pakistan-reading-project-wins-2020-international-prize-for-literacy-program-of-the-year/) but the page itself did not render the figures for us → unverified. Public micro-data for PRP regional EGRAs (2013–17) are catalogued at data.gov (e.g., https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/pakistan-reading-project-gilgit-baltistan-2013-2017).
  • Sindh Reading Program (SRP, USAID/GoS, 2014–19): 400,000 children, 8 districts; baseline (7,200 G2 students, 600 schools) found 28% non-readers in Sindhi-medium and 45% in Urdu-medium schools (STS International, https://sts-international.org/portfolio/sindh-reading-program/).
  • Sindh SELECT EGRA baseline (Apr–May 2024): 604 schools, 7,248 Grade 2 students, 12 districts, instruments "adapted under the SRP/PRP in 2014", administered on Tangerine (Sindh 2024 pp. 4, 14). Results (Urdu-medium unless noted):
  • Phoneme awareness: mean 5.29/10 (SD 3.98); 27% zero. Sindhi: 5.15/10, 29% zero.
  • Nonword reading: mean 13.39 cnwpm (range 0–50); 30% zero (rural 33%, urban 22%); non-zero readers ≈19 nwpm. Sindhi: 11.92, 41% zero.
  • Expressive vocabulary (10 action pictures): 8.75/10.
  • ORF: 19.25 cwpm on a 60-word passage (urban 24.63, rural 17.57; girls 20.35, boys 17.73); non-zero readers 34.81; 45% zero scores. Sindhi: 24.79 cwpm, 28% zero.
  • Reading comprehension: 1.19/5; 60% zero; 6% answered all five. Sindhi: 1.47/5, 46% zero, 9% met the 4-of-5 standard.
  • Listening comprehension (3 Q): 2.30/3, 5% zero. Sindhi 1.66/3, 14% zero.
  • Against Sindh (SELD) standards: 90–91% failed the nonword standard; <30% reached fluency/comprehension proficiency. (Sindh 2024 pp. iii–vii.)
  • Punjab / PESRP: Punjab's grade-3 Literacy & Numeracy Drive (LND) is a tablet-based monthly sample test by monitoring assistants (7–10 pupils per visit, MCQ-style in Urdu/English/Maths), not an EGRA and not a fluency measure (IEEE, https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8966738/). No published Punjab EGRA-Urdu cwpm series was found in this session (unverified/none found).
  • KP / Pashto: PRP operated in KP with Urdu materials; the only KP-specific paper found is an observational teacher-checklist study with no fluency data (Ilkogretim Online 2021, https://ilkogretim-online.org/index.php/pub/article/download/6293/6095/12076). Pashto EGRA cwpm figures: none found in accessible sources (they exist inside the PRP regional endlines above — unverified).

4.2 Benchmarks (cwpm) for Urdu and Sindhi

Set under the USAID project (PRP) by grade and language, three bands (ACR-Asia Table 2, p. 16):

Grade Urdu: does not meet / meets / exceeds Sindhi: does not meet / meets / exceeds
1 <30 / 30–60 / >60 cwpm <30 / 30–50 / >50
2 <60 / 60–90 / >90 <50 / 50–80 / >80
3 <70 / 70–100 / >100 <60 / 60–90 / >90

At the time of the ACR-Asia compilation only 20% of Urdu and 24% of Sindhi students met the grade-2 benchmark (ACR-Asia Table 5, p. 18). Sindh 2024 uses the same "50–80 cwpm" standard for Sindhi and quotes it for Urdu too; comprehension standard = 4 of 5 questions. Note these Urdu targets (60–90) are far above the 40–50 cwpm typical of Latin-script benchmarks; ACR-Asia (p. 8, citing Abadzi 2011) argues unvowelled scripts (Arabic, Farsi, Pashto, Urdu) may need a higher speed to comprehend, because readers hold alternative pronunciations in working memory.

4.3 Urdu-script adaptation issues (what the instrument must handle)

  • Contextual letter forms: each Urdu letter takes up to four shapes (initial, medial, final, isolated); Nastaliq's sloping baseline and stacked ligatures reduce clarity about where one letter ends and the next starts (r12a Urdu orthography notes, https://r12a.github.io/scripts/arab/ur). A letter-identification grid therefore shows isolated forms; whether PRP/SRP used letter names or sounds for Urdu is unverified (Sindh 2024 dropped letter naming for G2 as a ceiling task and used initial-sound identification instead).
  • Diacritics (aerab: zabar/zer/pesh, tashdid, jazm) are omitted in normal Urdu print, making Urdu a deep orthography; educational texts add them (r12a; DAWN, https://www.dawn.com/news/1924375). DAWN cites ASER: >40% of rural grade-3 pupils cannot identify letters and >55% of grade-5 pupils cannot read a grade-2 Urdu text. Design decision required: nonwords and passages with or without aerab — the two produce different cwpm and are not comparable. Sindh 2024 does not state which was used (unverified).
  • Nonword construction in Urdu: no published PRP rule found (unverified). The U-PASS Urdu phonological tele-assessment (Frontiers in Education 2025, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1572807/full) built Urdu non-words from Urdu's "mohmil" (nonsense compound) convention, selected Nastaliq over Naskh "for orthographic clarity", and controlled for aspirated and retroflex consonants — a usable template. Farukh & Vulchanova (2014, Dyslexia, "Predictors of reading in Urdu: does deep orthography have an impact?", https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4303915/) is the standard reference on Urdu orthographic depth (page blocked by CAPTCHA in this session — content unverified).
  • Direction and layout: right-to-left grids, 10 per row, need RTL stop-rule logic ("first line" = the top-right row).
  • Digraphs: aspirates written with do-chashmi he (e.g., بھ, پھ) and retroflexes (ٹ, ڈ, ڑ) must be counted as one letter-sound unit if a letter-sound grid is used (inference from Toolkit guidance on digraphs, p. 47 — design note, not a published Urdu rule).

4.4 ASER Pakistan vs EGRA

  • Structure: ASER language tool (Urdu/Sindhi/Pashto, and English) has five ordinal levels — Beginner/nothing, letters, words, sentences (Class 1 text), story (Class 2 text); "level 1 … not able to answer at all (Beginner), level 5 … perfect answer" (http://aserpakistan.org/tools). In the ASER Centre design the child reads 5 letters, 5 words, a 4-sentence paragraph, and a longer story; criteria are 4 of 5 letters, 4 of 5 words, paragraph/story read "with fewer than 3 mistakes", with a discontinue rule; administration ~10 min for reading+math, tested from the highest level down (Vagh 2012, https://img.asercentre.org/docs/Aser%20survey/Tools%20validating_the_aser_testing_tools__oct_2012__2.pdf; PSL adaptation guide, https://itacec.org/document/2019/december/Guide_and_Training_Manual_PSL.pdf). Math levels: number recognition (1-digit, 2-digit), subtraction with borrowing, division (3-digit by 1-digit).
  • Difference from EGRA: ASER is untimed, criterion-referenced (mastery/non-mastery), 5-item sampling, produces an ordinal level, not a rate. EGRA is timed, produces continuous rates (cwpm), and has component subtasks (phonemic awareness, decoding, listening).
  • Mapping (Hindi validation, n ≈ 20,000 across five samples): ASER-reading correlated r = .90–.94 with an EGRA/DIBELS-adapted Fluency Battery. Mean letter fluency by ASER level ≈ 2 (nothing), 17 (letter), 32 (word), 45 (paragraph), 62 (story); children at "word" level read the Grade-1 passage at ≈25 wpm, children at "paragraph" level read the Grade-2 passage at ≈44 wpm. Test–retest κ = .76 (reading); inter-rater κ = .64 simple, .82 weighted. 80–96% of children were classified consistently between the 5-item ASER subtests and the 52-item fluency grids (Vagh 2012, Tables 1–2). No Urdu-specific ASER↔EGRA mapping was found (unverified). Google's Read Along India pilot was evaluated with the ASER tool ("64% … showed an improvement in reading proficiency"; https://readalong.google/impact/).

4.5 Kiswahili (Tanzania) benchmarks — brief

Tanzania's 2014 national Standard-2 benchmarks: ORF 50 cwpm, nonwords 40 cnwpm, reading comprehension 80%; EGMA: A/S Level 2 80%, Missing Number 60% (TZ 2016 Table 5). 2016 national results (Std 2, 650 schools): ORF mean 23.6 cwpm (2013: 17.9), 16.1% zero; nonwords 15.7 cnwpm, 17.9% zero; syllables 39.9 per min; 5.3% "proficient" (met both ORF and comprehension benchmarks); proficient readers averaged 57.9 cwpm vs 18.8 for beginners (TZ 2016 pp. ii–iv, 31). Kenya's Tusome set two Kiswahili benchmarks, 17 cwpm (emergent) and 45 cwpm (fluent, 75% comprehension) (ACR-Asia p. 17). Kiswahili instruments use a syllable grid instead of letters, 5 comprehension questions (4 literal + 1 inferential), and dictation (TZ 2016 Table 1).

5. Digital and automated EGRA

5.1 Tangerine (RTI → Tangerine Central)

Open-source (GPL), offline-first Android/web app launched 2011 by RTI (Carmen Strigel, Amber Gove) to replace paper EGRA; used in 65+ countries; management transferred to Tangerine Central (women-owned) in 2025 (https://www.tangerinecentral.org/about; https://github.com/Tangerine-Community/Tangerine; https://www.rti.org/impact/tangerine-mobile-learning-assessments-made-easy). It implements the Toolkit protocol: assessor taps incorrect items, timer with red flash at 60 s, automatic time-remaining capture, auto-stop prompts, and prompts to ask only comprehension questions covered by text read (Toolkit protocol annex, tablet column). It does not score speech; a human still marks every item. Used in Sindh 2024 and Tanzania 2016.

5.2 Self-administered EGRA/EGMA (RTI + Imagine Worldwide, 2022–25)

Tablet, child-driven, no assessor scoring. Ghana English pilot (Sept–Oct 2022, 279 G2 pupils): "the tool does not currently have speech recognition capacity", so ORF is proxied by the Spelling subtask or a composite (https://shared.rti.org/content/report-self-administered-egraegma-pilot-ghana-english; https://www.tangerinecentral.org/impact/egra-egma-ghana-2022-analysis). Tanzania Kiswahili pilot (2025, 600 pupils G2–4, 15 schools): SA-EGRA/SA-EGMA "do not assess oral reading fluency … nor numeric automaticity", correlations with traditional EGRA/EGMA "reduced but generally acceptable" (threshold r ≥ .5), Cronbach α .87/.90, mean duration 51.8 min (TZ SA-EGRMA 2025 pp. ES-1, 10). Lesson: without ASR, the fluency construct is lost and the child-driven format is slow.

No Urdu child-speech reading corpus was found — the Xhosa recipe (record every item as a ~4 s clip, triple-mark, fine-tune a classifier) is the closest published path.

5.4 What has worked and what has failed (synthesis)

  1. Passage ORF with a large ASR model works surprisingly well for older readers (r ≈ 0.96 on Ghana teens with Whisper, no fine-tuning) but nothing equivalent is published for grade 1–3 children in a low-resource, non-Latin script.
  2. Letter- and nonword-level items are the hard case: off-the-shelf multilingual ASR essentially fails (6.9% exact match on Xhosa EGRA items); per-item fine-tuned classifiers reach ~91% — comparable to the 88–97% human inter-rater agreement seen in Sindh 2024, but only with 200–300 labelled clips per item.
  3. ASR over-detects errors (SERDA precision .31; Dutch MCC .43–.63), i.e., automated cwpm will be biased downward on weak readers unless the reference model includes expected miscues.
  4. Younger children are the residual error source in every child-ASR paper (Bambara <10; Tamil).
  5. Self-administered tablets without ASR had to drop ORF entirely and took ~50 min — evidence that the human/AI-listener, not the child-driven UI, is the right architecture.
  6. Comprehension answers: Henkel et al. report GPT-4 grading short-answer comprehension at QWK 0.92, above human raters (0.915) — LLM grading of spoken answers is plausible once transcribed.

6. Reliability practice — human enumerators, gold standards, and what it implies for an AI scorer

  • Selection threshold: "In an EGRA training, the benchmark is set at 90% agreement with the correct evaluation of the child for the final training assessment"; assessors below the benchmark are not selected (Toolkit §8.7, p. ~91). EGMA identically: assessors "who do not achieve a minimum of 90% agreement with the standard during IRR … will not be retained" (EGMA Toolkit p. 38).
  • How the gold standard is made: a technical expert plays "assessor" and a local expert plays "student" with scripted responses designed to contain the typical errors (hesitations, self-corrections, letter-name-for-sound, skipped lines, early-stop cases); all trainee assessors score the same performance simultaneously (en masse, live or video); this is done three times during the training week, with individual feedback after each (EGMA Toolkit §4.2.3; Toolkit §8.7).
  • Scoring the assessors (Toolkit Annex J): Assessor subtask score (%) = agreements with Gold Standard / items in subtask; item-level agreement across assessors is also computed; for timed subtasks an assessor is "in agreement" on time-remaining if within 2 seconds of the gold standard; overall percent agreement = mean of subtask and time-remaining agreements. Report by assessor, by subtask, and overall.
  • Field IRR during data collection (Toolkit §9.2 + Annex K; TZ 2016 Annex E): each day, two assessors per team double-score one extra child — one administers, the "listener" marks silently on a second tablet; discrepancies are reconciled. Tanzania 2016 reported ≈98% item agreement overall. Sindh 2024 ran 1,208 IRR paired tests on 7,248 assessments (17%); percent agreement ranged 88–97% — nonword reading highest (95% Urdu, 97% Sindhi), expressive vocabulary lowest (88% Urdu) (Sindh 2024 p. 15).
  • Instrument-level reliability: Fluency Battery test–retest .83–.98, α ≈ .93 (Vagh 2012); EGMA subtests α > .80 acceptable, word problems/Level-2 tasks weaker because of 5–6 items (EGMA Toolkit §3).

Implications for validating an AI scorer 1. The AI must be treated as one more assessor: score the same gold-standard scripted child performances and the same field double-scored children, and report percent agreement per subtask, per item, and on time-remaining (±2 s) in the Toolkit's Exhibit J format — this is the format ministry and donor teams already accept. 2. Pass bar: ≥90% item agreement with the gold standard per subtask (the enumerator bar), and field agreement in the 88–98% band observed for humans; for cwpm, agreement within the noise of human timing (±2 s ≈ ±3–4% of a 60 s window) and a mean bias near zero (SERDA-type error over-detection would fail a bias check). 3. Build the gold standard the way RTI does — scripted, error-laden performances, not only clean readings — so the AI is tested on hesitations, self-corrections, letter names given for sounds, skipped lines and auto-stop cases. 4. Multiple human markers disagree substantially at letter level (Xhosa: 71% expert agreement on all clips, 85% on consensus); use consensus-of-three labels for training/validation and report agreement against an expert EGRA reviewer, not against a single marker. 5. Keep the coach-in-the-loop marking as the primary record until the AI meets the bar per subtask and per language; publish disagreement rates by student subgroup (rural/urban, gender, weak readers) since ASR degrades on atypical speakers (Nese & Kamata).

Summary — 15 decision-relevant findings

  1. The Toolkit's fluency formula is score / (60 − time_remaining) × 60; the app must capture time-remaining to the second, and the human/AI must be within ±2 s of a gold standard to be "in agreement".
  2. All EGRA reading grids are 60 s, time-limited, with an early-stop after zero correct in the first line (10 letters / 5 words / first passage line); comprehension asks only questions covered by text actually read.
  3. Core EGRA = listening comprehension, letter ID, nonword reading, ORF + comprehension; familiar words and initial sounds are the usual additions; dictation/vocabulary are optional and weakly validated.
  4. Core EGMA = Number ID (timed), Number Discrimination, Missing Number (untimed, stop after 4 errors), A/S Level 1 (timed), A/S Level 2 and Word Problems (untimed, stop after 4); the child must say answers — pointing is explicitly insufficient, so audio (not tap) is the primary capture for every EGMA item.
  5. Published totals: EGRA 15–20 min (Toolkit), 10–15 min (Tanzania, 5 subtasks); EGMA ~20 min (+5 swap). A 5-minute per-child battery is a strict subset and must be re-benchmarked.
  6. Urdu grade-2 benchmark (USAID/PRP) is 60–90 cwpm "meets", >90 "exceeds"; Sindhi 50–80; only ~20–24% met it. Sindh 2024 Urdu-medium G2 mean ORF = 19.25 cwpm, 45% zero; comprehension 1.19/5, 60% zero.
  7. Sindh 2024 kept five subtasks (initial sound, nonwords, expressive vocabulary, 60-word passage + 5 Q, listening 3 Q) and dropped letter naming, familiar words, dictation — a defensible Urdu template already run on 7,248 children on Tangerine.
  8. Urdu adaptation decisions that change scores and must be fixed up-front: letter names vs sounds; isolated vs joined forms; passages/nonwords with or without aerab; RTL "first line" stop logic; digraph units (bھ, ٹ).
  9. ASER (Pakistan) is ordinal, untimed, 5-item; it maps onto EGRA fluency (r ≈ .90–.94 in Hindi; "story" level ≈ 44+ wpm on grade-2 text) but no Urdu mapping is published — we would need to create it.
  10. Kiswahili: TZ benchmark 50 cwpm (nonwords 40, comprehension 80%); 2016 national mean 23.6 cwpm, 5.3% proficient; Kenya Tusome 17/45 cwpm.
  11. Best automated ORF evidence: Whisper out-of-the-box, r = 0.96–0.98 with humans on Ghanaian teens; nothing equivalent exists for grade-1–3 children in Urdu, and child-speech WER for related scripts (Arabic) is ~66%.
  12. Letter/nonword items are where generic ASR fails (6.9% exact match on Xhosa EGRA clips); per-item fine-tuned classifiers reach ~91% with 200–300 labelled clips per item — a data-collection budget, not a model choice.
  13. ASR systematically over-flags reading errors on children (precision .31 in a 653-child Dutch validation), biasing cwpm downward for weak readers; a miscue-aware reference is required.
  14. Human bar to beat: 90% agreement with a scripted gold standard at training; 88–98% field IRR (Sindh, Tanzania). Validate the AI as "one more assessor" using the Toolkit's Exhibit-J agreement report, per subtask, on scripted error-laden performances and on double-scored field children.
  15. Coach-in-the-loop tap marking (Tangerine pattern) should remain the record of truth per subtask until the AI meets the bar for that subtask and language; the RTI self-administered tablets that dropped the human listener also lost ORF entirely and took 50 min.
