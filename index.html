EGRA in Five Minutes

We want a coach to test a child's reading and maths in five minutes with a phone, have the computer do the marking, and have the coach check the marks before anything is saved. This page explains what that means, what we already know, what we tested, and what we plan to build.

The idea in one minute

Today, to find out how well a child reads and counts, a trained person called an enumerator sits with the child for about 20 minutes, shows them letters, words, a story and some sums, and ticks right or wrong on a tablet. That is slow, needs a specialist, and only happens once or twice a year.

We want our coaches (the people who already visit schools to watch lessons) to do a shorter version with five children per visit. The coach records the child on the phone, the computer listens to the recording and fills in the marks, and the coach looks at the marks and corrects any that are wrong before they are saved. We keep both the computer's marks and the coach's corrections, plus the recording, forever.

Why bother? Because if the test is short enough to run every few weeks, we can see whether a child is improving during the year, not just at the end. And because a recording can tell us far more than a tick box: how fast the child reads, which words they trip on, whether they correct themselves, how long they pause. Nobody can write all that down live. A computer can.

Words you will meet

Every term is explained again where it first matters, but here they all are in one place.

EGRA
Early Grade Reading Assessment. A standard reading test for children in the first few years of school, designed by an American research group (RTI) and used in over 100 countries. It has several short parts called subtasks: naming letters, reading made-up words, reading real words, reading a short story for one minute, and answering questions about it.
EGMA
Early Grade Mathematics Assessment. The maths partner of EGRA: reading numbers, saying which of two numbers is bigger, quick addition and subtraction, and word problems.
ASER
The simpler test Pakistan uses today (run by ITA). A child is placed on a ladder: can they read letters, words, a paragraph, a story? Maths: numbers 1 to 9, 10 to 99, subtraction, division. It gives a level, not a speed. The call on 15 September said we want more detail than ASER gives.
Subtask
One part of a test, for example "read this list of words for one minute".
Timed window
A subtask where the child gets exactly 60 seconds. What matters is how many items they got right in that minute.
Stop rule
If a child gets nothing right in the first line, the tester stops that subtask so the child is not made to struggle for a full minute.
WCPM
Words correct per minute. The main reading-fluency number: how many words the child read correctly in one minute of a story. A Pakistani grade-2 child reading Urdu is expected to reach 60 to 90.
Enumerator
A trained tester who runs the test on a tablet. In the May 2026 study, 28 enumerators tested 2,233 children in 99 Rawalpindi schools.
Coach
Our school-visiting staff (also called TMs, AEOs, cluster coordinators depending on region). They watch lessons and give teachers feedback. We want them to run the five-minute test too.
Human in the loop (HITL)
The computer does the first pass, a person checks and corrects it, and the person's answer is final. Our lesson-observation feature already works this way: the AI fills a scoring form and the coach edits it.
Speech-to-text (STT), also called transcription
Software that turns a recording into written words. We use a service called Soniox; another is OpenAI's Whisper.
Timestamps
For every written word, the exact second in the recording where it was said. This lets us cut out "the 60 seconds after the coach said start".
Diarisation
Working out who is speaking when: the child or the coach.
Large language model (LLM)
An AI that reads and writes text, like ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude. Some can also listen to audio directly; we call those audio LLMs.
Reference text
The exact letters, words or story the child was supposed to read. The computer compares what it heard against this.
Alignment
Lining up the words the child said against the reference text to see which were read correctly, which were skipped, which were replaced by another word, and which were added. The classic method is called Levenshtein alignment (counting the smallest number of edits between two lists). Forced alignment is a stronger version that uses the audio itself, not just the transcript, and is what we need for reliable word-by-word marks.
Phoneme
A single speech sound. "Cat" has three: k, a, t. A pronunciation assessment service scores each one and can say "the child said eeli instead of Ali". Microsoft Azure and SpeechAce sell this for English. Nobody sells it for Urdu.
Item bank
The fixed list of test items (numbers, words, questions) with their correct answers, stored in our system so the computer knows what the child was asked.
Human floor
How often two trained humans agree with each other on the same recording. If humans only agree 55 percent of the time, we cannot expect a computer to agree with a human more than that; the floor is the realistic target.
Correlation (r)
A number from 0 to 1 saying how closely two sets of numbers move together. 0.97 means "almost the same ranking"; 0.3 means "barely related".
MAE
Mean absolute error. On average, how many words apart the computer's count and the human's count were.
Precision and recall (for word-by-word marks)
Precision: of the words the computer flagged as wrong, how many really were wrong. Recall: of the words that really were wrong, how many the computer caught. Low precision means lots of false alarms.
8 kHz vs 16 kHz
Recording quality. The May study's recordings were 8 kHz (phone-call quality). WhatsApp voice notes are 16 kHz (clearer). Every result on this page was measured on the worse quality.
R2
Our cloud file store (from Cloudflare). All recordings are saved there.
WhatsApp Flow
A form that opens inside WhatsApp. Our coaches already use one to check the AI's lesson-observation scores; the child-assessment checking screen will be another one.
TIP / LEAPS
A written placement test (Targeted Instruction Program, by the LEAPS research group) used in Islamabad and KP schools to sort children into ability groups. Paper, whole class at once, 50 minutes for maths. The "TIPS" folder you shared is this test.

How it is done today, and what we have to work with

The May 2026 study

In April and May, 28 enumerators tested 2,233 children in 99 Rawalpindi government schools with the full EGRA and EGMA in Urdu and English on tablets. The tablet software (SurveyCTO) recorded every session's audio and every tap: which letter the child got wrong, how many seconds were left on the timer, the answers to the questions. That whole export is in the Drive folder you shared, along with 207 of the recordings.

On top of that, seven reviewers later listened to 588 of those recordings and marked, subtask by subtask, whether the enumerator's score was right. So for each recording we have two human opinions: the enumerator's live marks and a reviewer's second opinion.

What the timing tells us

The middle child took 21 minutes: about 6 minutes of Urdu, 7 of English, 6 of maths. Three subtasks are 60-second timed reads that cost about 68 seconds each with instructions. Listening comprehension is slow because the enumerator reads a story aloud twice. The maths speed grid is the single longest part.

Why five minutes cannot mean "the same test, faster"

The child cannot read faster. The time saving has to come from three places: the coach stops tapping every item (the computer marks instead), we keep the stop rules so a child who cannot read letters is not made to attempt the story, and we choose which 60-second windows to keep. A full EGRA alone is 15 to 20 minutes by design, so a five-minute test is a deliberate selection, not a speed-up.

What happened when we tried a WhatsApp version already

In August we built a WhatsApp ASER tool. On 11 September the Rawalpindi field team said no: it asked one question per message, took 10 to 15 minutes per child, needed internet in schools that have none, and they did not trust the AI on Pakistani accents. Since then a "human decides" mode and a correction button have shipped. The one-question-per-message shape and the need for internet have not changed. This plan removes both.

What the team decided on 15 September

"I want to invest in a better test. I'm not happy with ASER. More granularity is the need of the hour."Haroon, alignment call, 25 minutes in

The call's recording was stuck in our meeting system (the row was marked private and the processing job had stalled), so I pulled the recording out and transcribed it. The decisions, with where they were said:

  • 30 minutes per coach per day, on top of the lesson observation. Five children at about five minutes each. Not released until it is proven under 30. Islamabad first; other regions two to three weeks later.
  • All three subjects (Urdu, English, maths), Urdu first. Comprehension questions stay.
  • The children are chosen by an algorithm, not the coach, so the sample is random. Still open: five new children each visit, or three new plus two we have tested before.
  • Based on EGRA, not ASER, and not the TIP written test unless someone shows it is better.
  • Break EGRA into small weekly quizzes for the WhatsApp student quiz, so class-level progress shows up weekly.
  • Use the recordings to check the tester too, the way our lesson-observation AI checks a lesson. The experiment below is the first run of exactly that.

The tests, explained

What each well-known test contains, how it is run, and what it is good and bad at, so you can judge which items we keep. Longer versions with sources are in the research folder.

EGRA

One tester, one child · 15 to 20 minutes · grades 1 to 3

Listen to a story and answer questions; name 100 letters in a minute; say the first sound of words; read 50 made-up words in a minute (this checks decoding without memory); read 50 real words in a minute; read a story for a minute and answer 3 to 5 questions. Stops early if the first line is all wrong. Result: letters and words correct per minute. Urdu versions exist.

Good: the speed numbers move within months, so progress shows. Bad: long; the letter part is the noisiest.

EGMA

One tester, one child · about 20 minutes

Read 20 numbers in a minute; say which of two numbers is bigger; fill the missing number in a sequence; quick addition and subtraction for a minute each; harder sums with paper allowed; six word problems. The child must say the answer, pointing does not count.

Good: separates children who know facts from children who count on fingers. Bad: some parts have only 5 items, so they are unreliable on their own.

ASER

One tester, one child · about 10 minutes for reading and maths together

A ladder: nothing → letters → words → paragraph → story. Pass a rung by getting 4 of 5 right. Maths: 1 to 9 → 10 to 99 → subtraction with borrowing → division. Result: a level name.

Good: cheap and governments understand it. Bad: a child can improve a lot and stay on the same rung, so you cannot see change during a term.

ICAN

One tester, one child · about 15 minutes · run in Pakistan in Urdu

26 maths items across numbers, operations, patterns, shapes, measurement and reading a chart. Gated: harder sets only if the easier one is passed.

Good: covers shapes and data that EGMA skips. Bad: nothing is timed, so no speed measure.

TIP (the folder you shared)

Written, whole class at once · maths is 40 questions in 50 minutes · grades 1 to 5

Urdu and English: match a picture to a word, join letters into a word, plurals, fill the verb, pick the picture for a sentence, fill gaps in a paragraph, write three sentences. Maths: sums with carrying, missing numbers, bigger or smaller, odd or even, times and division, fractions, word problems. Each class stops at its own page. Sorts children into KG / Class 1 / Class 2 / Class 3 groups.

Good: it is the grouping Islamabad and KP classrooms already use. Bad: it tests writing as much as reading; nothing is read aloud or timed; far too long for us. We should map our results onto its groups, not adopt it.

DIBELS (the American one-minute model)

One tester, one child · one minute per part

Letter naming, breaking words into sounds, made-up words, real words, a story, each in one minute. Proof that a full picture of a young reader fits in five minutes.

Good: the template for "one minute per skill". Bad: English only.

AI reading apps (Amira, Google Read Along, Microsoft Reading Progress)

Child reads to a phone or laptop

All produce the same things from a read-aloud: words per minute, accuracy, a list of mistakes by type, and a per-skill report for the teacher. Read Along has Urdu.

Good: shows what word-by-word output looks like. Bad: English first; their accuracy claims are not independently checked.

What governments already run here

Punjab LND, national NAT, Sindh's EGRA

LND: seven multiple-choice questions on a tablet for seven children per school per month. NAT: a grade-4 paper test. Sindh 2024: a government-owned Urdu EGRA on 7,248 children, five subtasks.

Good: legible to officials. Bad: none measures reading speed at scale.

What almost every test has in common. Five things: knowing letters, decoding (made-up or single words), reading a passage with a speed, answering questions on it, and recognising numbers plus basic sums. Everything else appears in only one test and can be dropped. Every short test gets short the same two ways: skip the hard rung if the easy one failed, and choose a subset. What a recording adds that paper never could: a speed on every item and a list of the exact mistakes.

What the coach does, step by step

Follow one child. Ayesha is in grade 3. Her coach has just finished recording a lesson in her class.

  1. The offer. The moment the lesson recording is received, Rumi (our WhatsApp assistant) sends: "Today's five children for assessment: Ayesha, Bilal, …" The list was chosen in advance by the sampling algorithm. The coach can accept or skip; the observation itself does not depend on it.
  2. The Urdu block, one voice note. The coach sits with Ayesha, holds up the printed Urdu story card, presses record, and says the cue phrase from the card: "یہ کہانی پڑھو۔ شروع۔" Ayesha reads for a minute; the coach says "بس" and asks the three questions on the card, then the five first-sound items. The coach stops the recording. One voice note, about two minutes.
  3. The English block, one voice note. Same pattern: story card, cue phrase, one minute of reading, two questions, then eight made-up words.
  4. The maths block, one voice note plus one photo. The coach shows eight numbers for Ayesha to read aloud, then runs the one-minute quick-sums strip aloud. Then the coach hands Ayesha a small sheet with four written sums and one word problem to do while the coach fetches Bilal, and photographs the sheet when she is done.
  5. The computer marks while the next child reads. Ayesha's three voice notes and photo upload when there is signal. If the school has no internet, they queue on the phone and go later. The computer's marks are ready in under a minute once uploaded.
  6. The checking screen. A form opens in WhatsApp showing the computer's marks for Ayesha, block by block: 31 words correct in 60 seconds, two of three questions right, and so on. The coach changes anything that looks wrong. Where the computer was unsure, the form says "listen and mark" with the clip attached, and the coach decides.
  7. Saved twice. We store what the computer said and what the coach said, side by side, plus the recordings. The coach's answer is the official one. The computer's answer is kept so we can measure how often it was right and improve it.

What the computer does with a voice note

Ayesha's Urdu voice note is two minutes long. Here is what happens to it, in order.

  1. Save the file. The voice note goes straight into our cloud file store (R2) under a name that says which school, session, child and block it is. We never rely on WhatsApp keeping it; WhatsApp deletes media after about a month.
  2. Turn speech into words with timestamps. A speech-to-text service (Soniox for Urdu, Whisper for English) writes out every word and the exact second it was said, and labels which words are the coach's and which are the child's. We tell it the language in advance; we learned the hard way that if we let it guess, it writes a child's accented English in Urdu letters.
  3. Find the windows. The computer looks for the coach's cue phrase ("یہ کہانی پڑھو") and the child's first word after it, and cuts out exactly 60 seconds from there. That is the story window. The questions come after "بس". Because the timestamps come from the speech-to-text service, the cut is accurate to the second. (We tested letting an audio AI do the cutting; its clock was off by 20 to 75 seconds, so it only gets to label, never to time.)
  4. Mark the words. The computer already knows the story text (the item bank). It gives the 60-second clip and that text to an audio model (Gemini 3.8 Flash), which returns a verdict per word: read correctly, wrong, or not reached. In the evaluation this landed within five words of the enumerator for three children in four; a second method, lining the transcript up against the text by spelling, runs alongside as the fallback and as a check on the chips.
  5. Mark the questions. The child's spoken answers to the three questions go to a text AI with the marking guide ("the answer should mention Bilal's father") and it returns right or wrong with its reason. It also notes which language the child answered in.
  6. Check the coach. The same transcript shows whether the coach read the story twice, whether they prompted the child inside the timed minute, and how noisy the room was. In our test this caught a slip that a human reviewer had missed.
  7. Say how sure it is. Every mark carries a confidence. Marks above the bar we set from the experiment below appear as numbers on the checking screen; marks below it appear as "listen and mark".
  8. Fill the form. The marks become a pre-filled WhatsApp form for the coach.

The experiment: does this actually work on real recordings?

We took every one of the 187 study recordings that has both a child's tablet scores and a reviewer's re-listen, and ran the candidate pipeline and every model we were considering over them. This is the "evaluation harness": a set of numbered scripts anyone can rerun, with every model reply cached. The full record, one table per task with every scorer we tried, is the evaluation page; twelve worked examples show, for real windows, the printed text, the clip, the transcript, the enumerator's marks and every model's per-word verdicts side by side. Total model spend for the whole evaluation was about $70.

Where the human scores come from

Two layers. The enumerator's live marks from the tablet: for every letter, word and story word, whether the child got it right, plus the counts and timer readings. And the reviewer's second opinion: someone listened to the recording afterwards and marked whether each of the enumerator's scores was right, wrong, or impossible to tell from the audio.

Why the "human floor" matters

Before judging any computer we asked how often the two humans agree with each other. On letters and maths, about nine times in ten. On the timed word lists and the story, only 51 to 62 percent of the time: the recordings are noisy phone-quality audio from real classrooms, and even a trained human cannot always tell what a child said. So on those tasks the fair target for a computer is "as good as the second human", not perfection. That floor is printed beside every number on the evaluation page.

Reading the results

Give the model the clip and the printed text, and counting is solved. Gemini 3.1 Pro and 3.8 Flash, listening to the 60-second clip with the exact text in front of them, land within five words of the enumerator for 74 to 97 percent of children, on poor audio, for one to six cents a window. Transcript-only methods, whether alignment or a text model, under-count by 8 to 20 words because speech-to-text drops words the child did say.

Per-word chips are usable as suggestions, not marks. The best model's flags are right two-thirds of the time on the Urdu word list and just over half on the story; every other scorer is well below that. Chips appear where the model and the alignment agree; elsewhere the coach decides.

Cheap models are not interchangeable with expensive ones here. GPT audio mini, MiMo, Nemotron and Voxtral were 12 to 23 words off; Gemini 2.5 Flash waved mistakes through. Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite is the surprise: nearly as good as 3.8 Flash on counts at a third of the price.

Three things the harness surfaced by accident

  • Our Microsoft Azure pronunciation service is dead: both stored keys are rejected, and the same key is on the live server, so English reading tests have quietly been falling back to a weaker method. Filed as an urgent bug; the fix is to swap that path to SpeechAce rather than regenerate the key.
  • Letting the transcription service guess the language breaks on accented child English: it wrote the child's English in Urdu and Devanagari script. Tell it the language.
  • The study export records 0 for both "read correctly" and "never reached", so per-word comparisons are only valid up to the word the enumerator says the child reached. The first pass of this evaluation got that wrong; the numbers here use the corrected rule.

What the scoring costs

About 10 cents per child for the full five-minute test with Gemini 3.8 Flash on every window plus the graders; about 25 cents with Gemini 3.1 Pro on the Urdu windows. Both are far below the roughly 43 cents a coaching session costs today.

The bigger point. Paper gives one number per subject per child per term. A recording gives a word table per child per visit. Four things become possible that never were: a growth curve for each child from repeated five-minute samples; spotting the mistake patterns across a whole class (what to teach next week); calibrating our own stories on thousands of real reads instead of formulas; and a running fairness check on every coach instead of a one-off training test. None of this needs new AI. It needs the word table stored from day one, with the coach's corrections next to it.

Maths: should the child speak the answer or write it?

A fair question is whether marking maths from speech is even normal. It depends on what the item is measuring, and the standard tests already split the same way.

Speaking is the norm for speed items. EGMA insists the child says the answer for number reading and for the one-minute sums, because these measure how quickly a child retrieves facts. A grade-1 child writes so slowly that a written version would measure handwriting, not maths. ASER and ICAN are spoken throughout. Our experiment confirmed spoken answers can be marked when the question is spoken too (word problems 0.70), and number reading becomes easy once the computer knows which numbers were shown, which it will in the product.

Writing is the norm for computation. EGMA lets the child use paper for harder sums; the TIP test, LND and NAT are entirely written; and the child's working (tally marks, carrying) is evidence a recording cannot capture. A photo also costs the coach nothing: the child writes six items while the coach fetches the next child.

So: both, split by what the item measures. Speak the number reading and the timed sums; write the two-digit sums and one word problem, photographed once. AI can read children's final answers from a photo well today; reading their working reliably is a version-2 goal, and we should test 50 real sheets before trusting either.

Why not simply photograph everything. Photographing everything would lose the two things only audio gives in maths: the per-minute speed and the sound of a child counting instead of knowing. Speaking everything would lose the working. The split above is what EGMA itself does.

The five-minute test, from first principles

Do not start by asking which EGRA parts to cut. Start by asking what a five-minute recording can contain. One 60-second story read gives speed, accuracy, mistake types, self-corrections and pauses; add three questions and it gives comprehension. That one window already carries more detail than the whole ASER ladder. So the rule is: spend the minutes on windows that yield many measures, use stop rules so every child works at their own level, and keep every timed window at the full 60 seconds so our numbers stay comparable with the published Urdu benchmarks. Detail comes from measuring more per window, not from squeezing in more windows.

Two departures from the original brief. Five minutes is the test, not the visit: walking a child in and out adds a minute, and the 30-minute promise absorbs it. And the 100-letter grid should be a fallback rung, not the default: it is where both humans and computers are weakest, and a child who can read the story has proven the letters.

Why this is more than "EGRA cut down to ASER". ASER gives one level per subject. This gives, per child per visit: two reading speeds with mistake lists, comprehension in two languages, a decoding check in each, a maths speed with a strategy signal, written computation, and a coach-quality score; plus a per-item table that builds up into class-level phonics gaps and growth curves over time. The checking screen

We reuse exactly how the lesson-observation form works today. The computer's answer is saved once and never changed (version 1). The WhatsApp form opens pre-filled from it. The coach's edits are saved as version 2 with a note of what changed. Only the coach who did the assessment can submit it.

Two changes for children: the form is per child, not per lesson, and each edit is saved item by item, because we need to know exactly where the computer was wrong in order to improve it.

Each screen is one block. Each subtask row shows the computer's count; the coach taps to change it. Where the computer was confident, the words it marked wrong appear as chips the coach can flip. Where it was not (letters, first sounds, and all per-word marks in version 1), the row says "listen and mark" with the clip attached. A "manual mode" switch lets a coach mark a whole block themselves; the audio is still recorded and marked in the background so we can keep measuring the computer.

Decisions to be made

D1
The five-minute test as designed above: two 60-second stories with fallback rungs, made-up words for readers, spoken maths speed plus a written photo strip. Or a variant. Shorter windows would break comparability and add less information than measuring more per window.
D2
Maths split by what the item measures (speak the speed items, write the computation). Photo-only loses the speed and the counting signal; speech-only loses the working.
D3
Urdu instrument choices: letter names or letter sounds (the May study used names; Sindh used first sounds); vowel marks (aerab) on or off in the stories; which two grade-2 stories; how to build the made-up words. Sabeena and Sameer's call, and the marker cannot be built until these are fixed.
D4
Retire Azure, use SpeechAce for English. The evaluation says SpeechAce's English per-word marks are the best we measured and it is the only scorer that names the mispronounced sound, so there is no reason to regenerate the dead Azure key. What needs deciding is the SpeechAce plan tier (the 30-second cap means two calls per window; the Pro tier raises it to 45 s). Live English reading tests stay on the weaker fallback until the swap ships.
D5
Sampling for the pilot: five new children per visit, or three new plus two repeats. The experiment has no opinion; the impact team's big EGRA every three months stays the official measure either way.
D6
Map our results onto the TIP groups (KG / Class 1 / 2 / 3) so Islamabad and KP teachers can use them directly. Small work, big legibility.
